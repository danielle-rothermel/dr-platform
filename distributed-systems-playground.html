How dr-platform lets many workers write one truth

An interactive tour of the kernel's submission, enqueue, worker, reconciliation, cancellation, and export flows — the race windows each one opens, and the exact mechanism that closes each window. Built from a full read of src/dr_platform/; every claim cites a file. Companion to the deep-modules design review.

§1The machine at a glance — every write funnels through claims, every read through cuts

The kernel's core bet: Attempts and Claims are append-only history, never mutable work slots. A worker never "takes a job" — it appends a leased Claim, commits a call-start fact before touching DBOS, and records the outcome with a compare-and-set. Anything uncertain stays uncertain until DBOS is observed authoritatively. Consumers see only the numbered flow below; everything in the middle band is what they don't have to think about.

§2Three interlocking state machines — click any state to see what pins it

One Attempt carries two orthogonal machines: enqueue_state (did the work reach DBOS?) and execution_state (what did DBOS do with it?). A third machine, the Claim disposition, mediates who is allowed to move the first one. All three are closed enums with SQL CHECK constraints tying them to their evidence columns — an Attempt physically cannot be claiming without a claim, terminal without terminal_at, or errored without a failure snapshot (db/schema.py:148-207).

§4Race gallery — every window the design opens, and the mechanism that closes it

The kernel's stance is consistent: pessimistic locks establish order, CAS predicates re-verify under lock, and uncertainty is persisted, not guessed away. Candidates are always loaded from an unlocked snapshot and re-validated after locking — the load-then-lock TOCTOU window is deliberate and closed by the re-check, never by hoping.

Two submitters replay one operation_key CLOSED

Window: the same key submitted twice with drifted inputs would fork one Operation's identity.
Mechanism: the manifest digest freezes items + recipes before any write; a replay must match every immutable field byte-for-byte (_validate_exact_replay, submission.py:898) or raise RegistrationConflictError. Equal replays are welcome — they resume.

Two registrars race the same page CLOSED

Window: a crashed submitter's successor could double-insert page items while the original limps on.
Mechanism: a registration lease (60s, renewed per page) plus a cursor CAS — the page commit requires registration_cursor == page_index AND lease_id == mine AND lease unexpired (submission.py:809-827). The lease is re-checked after the registration hook runs, so a stale registrar's whole page rolls back.

Two workers claim one pending Attempt CLOSED

Window: both would enqueue the same workflow and fight over the outcome.
Mechanism: claim selection holds a global exclusive advisory lock (CLAIM_SELECTION_ADVISORY_KEY, claims.py:1769) so pages never interleave, and the attempt CAS requires enqueue_state='pending' AND current_claim_id IS NULL (claims.py:196-225). Losing the CAS after holding the locks is treated as corruption, not retry.

Claim expires while its owner is mid-flight CLOSED

Window: a replacer steals the claim just as the slow owner finally calls DBOS → two live enqueuers.
Mechanism: the owner must first win the call-start CAS, which requires disposition='claimed' AND lease_expires_at > now (claims.py:485-506); replacement requires enqueue_call_started_at IS NULL (claims.py:312-336). Exactly one of the two CAS predicates can be true. A replaced owner gets LOST_AUTHORITY and stops.

Crash between call-start and outcome — two generals CLOSED

Window: did the DBOS call land? Re-enqueue blindly and you may run the work twice; give up and it never runs.
Mechanism: the claim is stranded at call_started; recovery observes DBOS by workflow_id (enqueue_runtime.py:468-591). EXISTING → adopt as workflow_already_present. ABSENT → replace the claim and re-fire; the deterministic SetWorkflowID makes even a wrong ABSENT verdict idempotent. UNCERTAIN → stay put, retry later.

Outcome lands but authority was lost — the leaked workflow CLOSED

Window: cancellation invalidates a call_started claim, but the in-flight DBOS call still creates the workflow: real work is running that the kernel decided shouldn't.
Mechanism: the claimant that discovers its success can't be recorded writes an enqueue_compensations row (claims.py:741-855); repair_late_enqueue_compensations cancels or observes the leaked workflow, needs 3 absence observations past a grace window to say "no workflow", and appends a single bounded hazard successor if it appears even later (cancellation.py:256-427).

Two reconcilers create the retry Attempt CLOSED

Window: both observe the same error, both insert Attempt N+1 → forked lineage.
Mechanism: both take the same per-workflow advisory locks (source and successor, sorted) before any row lock; then the Item pointer CAS UPDATE items SET current_attempt=N+1 WHERE current_attempt=N admits exactly one (reconciliation.py:1102-1113). Contract test proves outcomes [0,1].

Cancel races the finish line CLOSED

Window: operator cancels as the worker succeeds — which truth wins?
Mechanism: terminal wins, and terminal is immutable. Cancel re-inspects before firing; a SUCCEEDED/ERROR observation becomes disposition observed_terminal and the real result is kept (cancellation.py:594-605). Conversely a cancelled Attempt is sticky — a late SUCCEEDED observation is a no-op (reconciliation.py:808).

Cancelling a workflow someone else still needs CLOSED

Window: two Operations link the same workflow_id; cancelling one would kill the other's live work.
Mechanism: reference-aware skip — before the physical cancel, _has_other_reference scans for any other non-terminal current Attempt on that workflow; if found, the local Attempt finalizes skipped_shared and DBOS is never called (cancellation.py:1043-1077). Cancels are also non-recursive: child topology drift fails closed.

Workflow looks gone but is only slow CLOSED

Window: one ABSENT status read during a DBOS hiccup would tombstone live work as MISSING.
Mechanism: MISSING requires missing_observation_count ≥ 3 and ≥ 60s since the first absence (reconciliation.py:874-892), each observation guarded by an execution-state CAS. Once terminal, MISSING rows rotate through re-observation markers — re-checked forever, rewritten never.

Export reads while everyone writes CLOSED COSTLY — C2

Window: a snapshot that straddles a claim CAS would show an Attempt in two states across tables.
Mechanism: every writer opens with pg_advisory_xact_lock_shared(EXPORT_BARRIER); capture takes the exclusive form, opens REPEATABLE READ, allocates a change_seq high-water mark H while writers are excluded, reads WHERE change_seq ≤ H (export.py:785-866). Per-operation pins use platform_cut_version CAS (cut.py:49).

Two publishers promote the same bundle slot CLOSED

Window: a paused old publisher wakes after its lease expired and overwrites the new owner's pointer.
Mechanism: lease + monotonic fencing token; every effecting transaction's first statement is a gate CAS …WHERE owner=:run AND fencing_token=:token AND lease unexpired RETURNING mutation_epoch (publication.py:1291-1307) — a stale token rolls back everything, even already-staged DDL. Equal-snapshot losers commit truthful SUPERSEDED and never mint a receipt.

§5The lock ledger — five layers, one ordering discipline

Deadlock avoidance is by convention, applied everywhere: advisory locks before row locks; workflow locks sorted and deduplicated; row locks in fixed hierarchy order (operation → item → attempt → claim), each set sorted by key. External I/O (DBOS calls) is never performed while holding kernel row locks (cancellation.py:1310).

§6Concerns — correctness is bought with serialization; here's where the bill arrives

Nothing below is a correctness bug — the race gallery closes cleanly. These are places where the chosen mechanisms cap throughput or invite operational surprises as item counts and worker counts grow, plus one primitive that exists but isn't enforced. Severity is about scale behavior, not today's workloads.

C1 · Every enqueuer in the fleet funnels through one global lock — with Python callbacks inside it THROUGHPUT CEILING

claim_pending_attempts takes CLAIM_SELECTION_ADVISORY_KEY exclusively (claims.py:141), then — still holding it — runs the admit_targets Python callback (target resolution + DBOS queue validation), acquires one advisory lock per workflow and one FOR UPDATE query per attempt sequentially, and inserts/CASes up to 500 rows. Adding kernel workers adds zero claim throughput; a slow resolver or a network blip inside the critical section stalls every operation in the system, not just its own.

Relief: the lock exists to keep scheduling order globally fair — but fairness only needs serialization per scheduling domain. Sharding the key (per operation, or per service_class) or moving admission outside the lock (validate targets, then lock, then re-check) preserves the CAS safety story while letting independent operations claim in parallel. SELECT … FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED is the idiomatic Postgres alternative for exactly this shape.

C2 · Export capture holds the exclusive barrier through the entire snapshot read — a stop-the-world pause GLOBAL WRITE STALL

The comment says the cut is allocated "while writers are excluded" — but the code releases the barrier in finally, after reading every member table (export.py:800-864). Every kernel write transaction blocks on its shared barrier acquisition for the full duration of the capture. As item_attempts grows, snapshot reads grow, and the whole fleet's write path pauses proportionally — submissions, claims, reconciliation, cancellations, everything.

Relief: the barrier's job is done once two things are true: the RR snapshot is pinned and H is allocated with writers drained. Releasing the advisory lock immediately after nextval (the first reads already pin the snapshot) would shrink the global stall from O(snapshot size) to O(drain + one statement), with the same consistency guarantee. Worth a careful proof, but the ordering argument in the code's own comment supports it.

C3 · Operation rollups recompute from scratch on every outcome — O(items²) per phase SCALING CLIFF

_refresh_operation_enqueue_aggregate (claims.py:1581) re-reads the enqueue state of every current Attempt in the Operation — inside the outcome transaction, holding the operation row lock — each time one outcome is recorded. Reconciliation's _refresh_operation_lifecycle does the same per observation. A 100k-item Operation performs ~100k × 100k row visits across its enqueue phase, serialized on the operation row lock — so the rollup isn't just slow, it lengthens every claim/outcome transaction's critical section.

Relief: the counts are already columns on operations; incremental count = count + 1 transitions (the row lock is already held, and every state change knows its from/to) would make each outcome O(1). Keep the full recompute as a repair/audit path rather than the hot path.

C4 · The throttle tables exist, but nothing in the kernel enqueue path reads them UNENFORCED PRIMITIVE

backoff.py maintains throttle_state with exponential backoff, deterministic jitter, and operator holds — but _pending_candidates (claims.py:1022) and the reconciliation candidate queries never join it. blocked_until gates nothing; a rate-limited target keeps getting claimed and re-fired at full speed unless the application remembers to call delay_until_unblocked_seconds itself. That's exactly the class of "infra consumers shouldn't have to think about" that the rest of the kernel absorbs.

Relief: either wire it in (candidate queries exclude targets whose blocked_until/hold_until is in the future — one extra join) or document loudly that throttling is an application contract, not a kernel behavior. The half-state is the trap.

C5 · Retries have no pacing — a failing target is re-hit as fast as reconcile() loops RETRY STORM

An error observation with a retryable class creates Attempt N+1 immediately pending (reconciliation.py:1060) — no delay, no jitter, no consultation of C4's throttle. Enqueue-error resets are likewise immediate. Bounds are small (max_attempts=3, max_enqueue_tries=3) so a single Item can't loop forever, but a 50k-item Operation against a rate-limited provider will burn all its attempts in a tight burst — precisely when the provider needs backoff. And wait_operation makes every waiting client an extra reconciler driving that burst, all serializing through C1's lock.

Relief: a not_before timestamp on the successor Attempt (derived from the failure class via the existing backoff math) that candidate selection respects. All the pieces exist; they're just not connected.

C6 · A registration hook slower than its lease can never commit — silent livelock OPERATIONAL TRAP

The caller-owned RegistrationHook runs inside the page transaction, and page authority is deliberately re-validated after it returns, against a fresh clock (submission.py:735-742, with the CAS predicate re-checking lease_expires_at > clock_timestamp()). Correct — a stale registrar can't commit. But if a hook consistently takes longer than registration_lease_seconds (default 60), every attempt does all the work, fails the CAS, rolls back, and the next submitter repeats it: full effort, zero progress, no distinctive error — it surfaces as the generic "registration cursor CAS lost after page validation".

Relief: detect the signature (lease expired during hook execution, cursor unmoved) and raise a dedicated "hook exceeded lease — raise registration_lease_seconds or shrink page_size" error. One timing comparison at the failure site turns a livelock into a configuration message.
